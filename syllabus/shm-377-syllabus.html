Course Description

Management of hazardous materials, including best practices and federal regulations associated with transportation, storage, use, and disposal of hazardous materials in the workplace.

Prerequisites: CHEM 101 or CHEM 111/111LAB or CHEM 181/181LAB.

SHM 377 develops the scientific, quantitative, regulatory, and professional reasoning needed to manage hazardous materials in workplace settings.

The course examines hazardous materials across their lifecycle, including transportation, storage, use, exposure, release, emergency response, waste management, and disposal. Students will work with scientific information, measurement data, calculations, technical resources, regulations, and real or realistic hazardous materials problems to develop and defend management decisions.

Course Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  1. Anticipate, recognize, evaluate, and develop control strategies associated with hazardous materials.
  2. Utilize federal regulations related to hazardous materials to assess compliance.
  3. Utilize Department of Transportation regulations related to hazardous materials to assess compliance.

Course Organization and Approach

Hazardous materials management requires more than identifying a chemical or memorizing a regulation.

Professionals must be able to determine what material is present, understand how it behaves, determine where it may move, evaluate how it can be measured, interpret available data, identify applicable requirements, and decide how the material should be managed.

SHM 377 therefore uses an applied, case based approach that integrates science, exposure assessment, quantitative analysis, regulations, and management decisions.

Throughout the course, we will repeatedly use the following hazardous materials reasoning framework:

Material
   ↓
Phase / Form
   ↓
Medium
   ↓
Movement
   ↓
Properties
   ↓
Measurement
   ↓
Data
   ↓
Decision

The purpose of the framework is to help answer a larger series of professional questions:

What is the problem?
        ↓
What information do I need?
        ↓
Where can I find that information?
        ↓
What does the evidence tell me?
        ↓
What requirements apply?
        ↓
What should we do?
        ↓
How can I defend and communicate that decision?

The goal is not simply to memorize chemical properties, equations, regulatory citations, labels, or tables. The goal is to learn how to find and use the information needed to make a defensible hazardous materials management decision.

Lecture and Laboratory Integration

SHM 377 meets Monday and Wednesday in extended instructional periods.

Where appropriate, the two meetings will function as an integrated lecture and laboratory sequence:

Monday
Concepts / Case Framing / Preparation
                ↓
Wednesday
Laboratory / Data / Application / Decision Making

Monday meetings may introduce the scientific concepts, hazardous materials problem, technical tools, quantitative methods, or regulatory resources needed for an application.

Wednesday meetings may provide opportunities to apply those concepts through hazardous materials scenarios, calculations, regulatory research, measurement data, data analysis, interpretation, and management decisions.

Not every Wednesday meeting will produce a graded laboratory report. Class meetings may include guided practice, calculations, regulatory navigation, data exploration, Safety Data Sheet interpretation, case studies, group problem solving, project development, and other applied activities.

Lectures and laboratories are intended to reinforce one another rather than operate as separate components of the course.

Role of Chemistry

Chemistry provides tools for understanding hazardous materials behavior.

SHM 377 builds upon prerequisite chemistry knowledge without functioning as a second general chemistry course. Chemical and quantitative concepts will be introduced or revisited when they help us understand or solve a hazardous materials management problem.

Relevant concepts may include:

  • Density
  • Specific gravity
  • Mass and volume
  • Concentration
  • Unit conversions
  • Parts per million
  • Molarity
  • Volatility
  • Solubility
  • Vapor pressure
  • Chemical compatibility
  • Reactivity
  • Chemical quantities in containers
  • Regulatory or reporting quantities

Calculations are used to support interpretation and decision making rather than as isolated mathematical exercises.

Role of Regulations

Regulations are professional resources rather than lists of facts to memorize.

Students will practice locating, navigating, interpreting, and applying regulatory requirements to hazardous materials problems.

Relevant regulatory frameworks may include requirements administered by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, U.S. Department of Transportation, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, and other applicable federal or state agencies.

Students will increasingly practice determining:

What agency has authority?
        ↓
What regulation or technical resource applies?
        ↓
Where is the relevant requirement?
        ↓
What does it require?
        ↓
Does the scenario comply?
        ↓
What action is needed?

Data Analysis and Analytical Tools

Data are one source of evidence used to evaluate hazardous materials problems.

Students may work with environmental monitoring data, exposure measurements, inventories, incident data, regulatory information, calculations, or other quantitative evidence throughout the course.

R and RStudio are introduced early so that students gain experience using a reproducible analytical environment to import, inspect, summarize, visualize, and interpret a real environmental dataset.

SHM 377 is not an R programming course.

After the introductory R experience, students may use R, Excel, or another appropriate analytical interface for graded laboratory work unless a particular assignment requires a specific method.

Regardless of the tool selected, students remain responsible for performing the analysis correctly, documenting their work, interpreting the results, and explaining how the evidence supports their conclusions.

The guiding principle for analytical work in SHM 377 is:

Support the mechanics. Assess the reasoning.

Graded Laboratories

SHM 377 includes four major graded laboratories during the quarter.

Each laboratory is worth 25 points, for a total of 100 laboratory points and 50% of the course grade.

The laboratories progressively reduce instructional scaffolding while increasing student responsibility for hazardous materials reasoning, analysis, interpretation, and management decisions.

Graded Lab 01

Virtual Lab 01: Introduction to R & Environmental Data is the first graded laboratory.

Lab 01 is intentionally highly guided and completion based. Students who complete and submit the required materials receive full credit.

The purpose of Lab 01 is to establish a common analytical and reproducible workflow rather than assess previous programming experience.

Students will use R and RStudio to organize project files, create and save an R script, import environmental monitoring data, inspect a dataset, perform basic transformations and descriptive analyses, create a visualization, interpret results cautiously, and preserve a reproducible record of their work.

Lab 01 will remain available as a reference throughout the quarter.

Laboratories 02 through 04

Later laboratories will apply scientific, quantitative, analytical, regulatory, and professional reasoning to hazardous materials problems.

Students may use R, Excel, calculations, regulatory resources, technical references, or other appropriate tools depending on the problem and assignment requirements.

Later laboratories will progressively provide less procedural guidance and require greater independence in determining what information is needed, selecting appropriate tools, interpreting evidence, and developing management recommendations.

Major graded laboratory work will normally culminate in a professional written report. Assignment specific requirements and due dates will be provided through Canvas.

Laboratory Report Expectations

Laboratory reports should be independently produced unless an assignment explicitly states otherwise.

Students may discuss concepts, methods, and troubleshooting with classmates, but each individual laboratory submission should reflect the student’s own analysis, interpretation, calculations, and written work.

Depending on the problem, a laboratory report may include:

  1. Scenario or problem
  2. Hazardous material or process
  3. Available evidence
  4. Analysis
  5. Results
  6. Interpretation
  7. Technical and regulatory considerations
  8. Management recommendations
  9. Limitations
  10. References

The exact structure may vary depending on the assignment.

Regardless of the analytical tool used, students should document their work sufficiently for the analytical process and reasoning to be evaluated.

Tables and figures should be clearly labeled. Graphs should include informative axis labels and appropriate units. Units should be included in calculations and final answers. Numerical results should be interpreted in the context of the hazardous materials problem rather than reported without explanation.

Students should not submit unexplained computer output in place of written interpretation.

Final Group Project

The final group project is worth 100 points and accounts for 50% of the total course grade.

The project serves as the culminating application of the SHM 377 hazardous materials reasoning framework.

Students will work in teams to investigate a complex hazardous materials management scenario provided by Professor Rodriguez. The scenario will require students to integrate multiple stages of hazardous materials management rather than solve a single isolated problem.

Depending on the scenario, teams may need to consider:

  1. Material characterization
  2. Receiving and acquisition
  3. Storage
  4. Use and handling
  5. Exposure and release potential
  6. Controls and safety protocols
  7. Emergency considerations
  8. Waste generation and management
  9. Transportation and shipment
  10. Disposal
  11. Regulatory requirements
  12. Management recommendations
  13. Professional communication

Students will determine what information is needed, locate appropriate technical and regulatory resources, evaluate available evidence, perform appropriate analyses where necessary, identify limitations or missing information, and develop defensible management recommendations.

Project work will develop throughout the quarter. Selected laboratory periods will provide dedicated time for groups to work together on their projects. During this time, students may conduct research, locate and interpret technical or regulatory resources, analyze information, develop their recommendations, prepare their presentation, ask questions, receive feedback, and collaborate with other groups when appropriate.

These project work periods are intended to provide structured time for students to apply concepts from the course as they are introduced rather than completing the entire project at the end of the quarter.

Project deliverables may include a professional written report, figures and tables, applied calculations, data analysis, regulatory analysis, management recommendations, and an oral presentation.

Communication and Professional Expectations

Hazardous materials professionals must be able to communicate scientific, quantitative, regulatory, and technical information to workers, employers, regulators, communities, emergency responders, and other professionals.

Throughout SHM 377, students will practice communicating technical information through discussion, laboratory reports, case analysis, management recommendations, and the final project.

Clear communication is more important than unnecessarily complicated language. Students should work toward explaining hazardous materials problems accurately, supporting conclusions with evidence, and communicating recommendations in a way that the intended audience can understand.

Use email or Canvas for short questions, clarification, absence notifications, and meeting requests. Complex questions about course performance, personal circumstances, laboratory work, project planning, or grades are usually better handled during office hours or an appointment.

When emailing, identify the course and make the question clear. A useful subject line is SHM 377 Question About [Topic or Assignment]. Students are responsible for checking Canvas announcements and CWU email regularly.

Artificial Intelligence

Artificial intelligence is not prohibited in SHM 377. Students are encouraged to use AI thoughtfully as a tool for learning, problem solving, coding assistance, brainstorming, troubleshooting, and improving their understanding.

Students remain responsible for the accuracy, interpretation, reasoning, calculations, regulatory information, and recommendations contained in work they submit. AI should support rather than replace your own thinking.

If AI materially contributes to a laboratory report, project, analysis, or other submitted work, briefly identify the tool and explain where or how it was used.

Students should verify factual claims, calculations, regulatory information, code, and sources rather than assuming generated content is accurate. Students should also be able to explain the work they submit, including analyses or code developed with AI assistance.

Do not enter confidential, proprietary, sensitive, employer owned, or otherwise restricted information into AI systems.

AI availability and acceptable use vary among workplaces. Students should therefore develop sufficient understanding of the underlying analytical methods, hazardous materials principles, and professional resources to evaluate AI generated information and work without AI when necessary.

Using AI to fabricate evidence or citations, misrepresent generated work as your own independent work, or bypass the learning purpose of an assignment may constitute academic dishonesty. If you are unsure whether a use is appropriate, ask before submitting the work.

Academic Integrity

Students are expected to act in accordance with CWU academic integrity and student conduct requirements. Submitted work must accurately represent your own contribution and must appropriately acknowledge the work, ideas, evidence, and tools used.

Academic dishonesty may be addressed under CWU 501-20 and WAC 106-125-020.

Classroom Community and Conduct

CWU expects every member of the university community to contribute to an inclusive and respectful culture for all in its classrooms, work environments, and at campus events.

Hazardous materials management involves real workers, communities, industries, regulations, environmental releases, emergencies, and sometimes difficult events. We may disagree about interpretation, risk, compliance, or management strategies.

Students should question ideas and evidence without attacking people. Asking someone to explain or support a position is part of academic inquiry.
